I was scrolling through my feed recently and caught an exchange between Victoria Derbyshire and Professor Geoffrey Hinton, often called the Godfather of AI.

- **Victoria Derbyshire:** "You just said 10% does not seem an unreasonable estimate that AI could kill all humans?"
- **Geoffrey Hinton:** "Yes."
- **VD:** "How could it kill us all?"
- **GH:** "If it is much smarter than us... design biological viruses, devastating cyber attacks, there are just countless ways it could get rid of us if it wanted to."

It stops you in your tracks when the guy who helped build the engine warns about the crash. It sounds like pure sci-fi horror, and it definitely makes you wonder.

When I step back and look at it properly, I realize almost every major leap in human capability triggers this exact same pattern of fear. *Again* and again.

## We've been here before

- **Fire:** Early civilizations feared controlled flames so much that ancient laws like the Code of Hammurabi, around 1750 BC, imposed death penalties for careless domestic fire use after fires levelled whole wooden towns.
- **Trains:** In the 1830s, medical experts warned that traveling over 20 mph would rupture human lungs and cause permanent brain damage.
- **Electricity:** In the 1880s, public campaigns claimed alternating current in homes was purely an execution tool, pointing to 1,000-volt state executions as proof it was too lethal for living rooms.
- **Automobiles:** British law enforced the Red Flag Act, requiring a man to walk in front of cars waving a red flag, because driving over 15 mph was expected to cause instant public insanity.
- **Calculators:** In the 1970s, educators protested hand-held calculators, predicting a generation of children who would completely lose the ability to perform mental arithmetic.
- **Mobile phones:** In the 1990s, global news reports claimed holding cell phones against your head cooked brain tissue with radiation, while schools banned them to prevent total social collapse.

## What history actually tells us

Hinton is clearly not stupid and knows his onions. However, history shows me that humanity does not get wiped out by its own inventions. Powerful tools bring real risks, and messy transition periods always cause disruption. But we adapt. We set standards. We figure out how to wire the house safely instead of sitting in the dark.
