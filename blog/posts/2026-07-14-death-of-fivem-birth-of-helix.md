In March, FiveM hit 202,756 concurrent players on Steam. A new record, and only a partial picture, because everyone playing through the Rockstar Launcher, Epic, or a direct download is not counted.

For a mod that was, less than three years ago, the subject of DMCA takedowns from the publisher that now owns it, that is a strange place to land.

It is also, almost certainly, the high-water mark.

## How FiveM got here

FiveM started life as an unofficial multiplayer mod for GTA V. A way for players to run their own custom servers outside of GTA Online. For most of the last decade it lived in a grey zone: enormously popular, monetised through Patreon and escrow systems, and tolerated by Rockstar because of the goodwill the roleplay community generated.

The flagship servers are the reason it matters. [NoPixel](https://www.nopixel.net/) is the household name. A heavily moderated roleplay city that turned GTA RP into a Twitch genre of its own, complete with audition queues and a tier of streamers built entirely on its characters. [District 10 RP](https://www.district10rp.com/) is one of the newer wave of community-run cities chasing the same model. Whole creator economies sit on top of these servers: developers, scripters, asset shops, voice actors, moderators. None of that exists without the platform under it.

That changed in August 2023, when Rockstar acquired Cfx.re, the team behind FiveM and RedM. The framing at the time was generous: bring the mod community in-house, formalise the ecosystem, prepare the ground for GTA VI.

The story since has been less generous to the original team. By mid-2024, every founding Cfx.re member had left or been pushed out. The community kept running. That part was always bigger than any individual. But the project changed hands quietly.

In December 2025, FiveM launched on Steam. In January 2026, the official Cfx Marketplace went live, with asset bundles up to $389.99. And in early 2026, Take-Two issued a shutdown notice to alt:V, the main competing GTA V multiplayer mod, with a final closure deadline of 6 July 2026.

If you were reading the moves, the direction was clear: one official platform, one official marketplace, one set of terms.

## The creators carrying it

The thing that made FiveM more than a chatroom with cars was the modders.

Scripters, 3D artists, animators, anti-cheat developers, full-framework teams. Some run as small studios, many are solo. Between them, they have rebuilt the GTA V engine into something Rockstar never shipped: persistent identity, full economies, real estate, faction systems, anti-cheat layers, telemetry, custom UI frameworks. The big servers don't really run "FiveM". They run a stack of paid and free assets built by hundreds of contributors. Some of these teams have transformed both the *experience* and the *security* of the servers they ship for.

There is real money in it. A premium framework or anti-cheat package can run hundreds of dollars per server licence. A custom map or vehicle pack can run thousands. Creators have built livelihoods inside this market.

And then there is the other half of the scene: the leak forums.

Premium scripts get pirated almost as fast as they ship. Entire sites exist solely to host leaked paid assets, from server frameworks to anti-cheat tooling to asset packs, available to any operator who would rather not pay. Server hosts then run leaked scripts in production, sometimes openly, sometimes badly disguised. The result is a permanent cat-and-mouse: creators roll out new licensing schemes, watermarks, hardware-fingerprint checks, kill-switches; the leakers crack each new measure, re-upload, repackage.

The closest analogue is the anti-cheat arms race in games like CS2. Every mitigation lasts weeks, sometimes days, before the workaround appears. The cost is borne by the creators, and a lot of solo modders eventually walk.

## The GTA 6 squeeze

GTA VI launches in autumn 2026. Industry chatter points at *Project ROME*, the Rockstar Online Modding Engine, as the official first-party creator platform that will ship alongside it. ROME is the long game. FiveM is the bridge.

You can see the logic. Owning the modding community before the new title lands lets Rockstar set the monetisation rails, define licensing terms, route creator IP through their own marketplace, and close the legal grey zone FiveM operated in for a decade.

For Rockstar, that is good business. For the independent creators who built businesses on top of FiveM, the server owners, asset shops, and roleplay communities, it is a slow squeeze. You can keep building, but the platform is no longer yours, the marketplace takes a cut, and the platform itself is scheduled to be superseded by the publisher's own tools in eighteen months.

That is a difficult place to invest creative time.

## The crime-and-ego loop

There is another problem with FiveM roleplay, and it has nothing to do with Rockstar.

Most of the big servers run the same template: cops, criminals, and the slow grind of trying to look more important than the person next to you. The dominant genre is crime and ego, and hours go into boosting both. It works for a slice of the audience, and very well for streamers, because conflict and status are easy content. But it leaves a much bigger audience on the table.

Not everyone wants to be a gangster or a police officer. Roleplay as a medium can do far more: slower, more human, more consequence-driven. *What goes around comes around* is a richer engine than *whose crew is more feared this week*. You can find that kind of play in pockets, but the centre of gravity pulls in the other direction. The loudest servers, the [District 10 RP](https://www.district10rp.com/)s of the scene, set the tone, and the streamers and onboarding flow reinforce it.

A clean platform is also a chance to set a new tone. Whether anyone takes that chance is a separate question.

## Enter Helix

[Helix](https://helixgame.com/) is the solution to clean this up and give the modders and players alike a new home now FiveM is starting to crumble.

Built on Unreal Engine 5 by Hypersonic Laboratories Inc., a $7M-funded studio, it positions itself directly against FiveM's limitations. The pitch: no takedowns, no licensing ambiguity, full IP ownership for creators, and a creator stack designed for roleplay from day one.

The feature list reads like a wishlist FiveM server owners have been writing for years:

- UE5 visuals out of the box, not bolted on
- A no-code Build Mode for world creation
- Scripting in Lua, TypeScript, or Blueprint
- A Creator Kit built on the full UE5 editor
- A marketplace (the Helix Vault) where creators keep their IP
- Free to play for users

It is currently in Closed Alpha. There is no release date. That last part matters.

## The producer take

Creator platforms live and die on critical mass. FiveM has a decade of momentum, a generation of streamers who built audiences inside it, and a marketplace that, whatever you think of Rockstar's terms, has a real economy attached. Helix has a clean engine, a clean licence, and if built right, a new base for roleplay modders and its ever-growing community.

Two questions decide which way this goes.

**First: can Helix hit a usable beta before Project ROME's official tools land alongside GTA VI?** If ROME ships with first-party support, a polished creator suite, and the GTA brand attached, the addressable market for an alternative shrinks fast. If Helix is in open beta by then, with a credible content library and a few flagship servers, the conversation looks very different.

**Second: do creators value freedom enough to switch?** "No takedowns, you own your IP" is a powerful pitch when you have just watched the team that built your platform get bought, eased out, and replaced. It is a less powerful pitch than "thousands of people are already on it." The roleplay scene tends to follow the streamers. Whichever platform NoPixel-scale operators pick, the audience will follow.

My guess is stronger than that. Look at what is already lined up. The testers are there, drawn in by the closed alpha. The end users are there, watching where FiveM is heading and not liking it. The developers are there, tired of seeing their scripts pirated and their platform tightened from above. The modders and the artists are there, looking for somewhere their IP is actually theirs.

The demand is not the problem. The platform is. As soon as Helix is genuinely ready, people will move.

For those old enough to remember, this is the Field of Dreams principle. *[Build it and he will come.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Ay5GqJwHF8)*
